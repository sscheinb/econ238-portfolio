Four Times the Economy, Under a Quarter of the Air Pollution

By Sam Scheinbach

Does a bigger economy have to mean dirtier air? EPA's numbers for the United States say no, with some catches.

Gross domestic product+349%
Emissions, six principal air pollutants−78%
Vehicle miles traveled+197%
Population+67%
Energy consumption+46%

Change in the United States from 1970 to 2025. Emissions are the combined tons of carbon monoxide, nitrogen oxides, sulfur dioxide, volatile organic compounds, particles and lead. Source: EPA, Air Quality: National Summary, which draws on BEA, FHWA, Census and EIA data. [12]

The main finding

Between 1970 and 2025, GDP rose 349%, vehicle miles traveled rose 197%, population grew 67% and energy use grew 46%. Over the same 55 years, EPA's estimate of combined emissions of six principal air pollutants fell 78%. Emissions are now 22% of their 1970 level while the economy is about 4.5 times its 1970 size. [1]

Divide one by the other and each dollar of output carries about 5% of its 1970 emissions. The same arithmetic gives drops of roughly 93% per vehicle mile and 87% per person. Over these 55 years, more output did not mean more of these pollutants.

The other side

The good news has limits. About 115 million people still live in counties above at least one national air standard, and coarse particle levels are up 23% since 2010. [1] The headline number also counts only tons emitted inside US borders, so pollution that moved overseas with factories would look like progress. One study linked US and western European consumption to more than 108,600 premature deaths in China in 2007. [8, 9] The total is also a sum of tons, so heavy pollutants dominate it, and carbon dioxide is not one of the six.

A cleaner economy is not the same as clean air for everyone.

Some pollutants vanished. Others barely budged.

Sulfur dioxide and lead are nearly gone. Ozone-forming chemicals and particles have fallen far less.

In the air people actually breathe, sulfur dioxide is down 94% and carbon monoxide 81% since 1990, but ozone is down just 24%. [1]

Why it happened: rules, markets and a new part for cars

Air pollution is an externality: the people who emit it do not pay for the harm it does to everyone else, so markets alone give them little reason to cut it. That is why the policies below used rules, caps and tradable permits.

  • 1970sEPA phases lead out of gasoline and the catalytic converter arrives. Lead in the air fell 98% from 1980 to 2014, and new cars are 98 to 99% cleaner than in the 1960s. [6, 7]
  • 1990Congress creates the first national cap and trade program, for power plant sulfur dioxide. By 2019 those emissions were 94% below 1990, at costs far lower than expected. [4, 5]

Economists split growth's effect on pollution into scale, composition and technique, and a well-known 1995 study found no evidence that environmental quality steadily worsens as economies grow. [10] This page shows the outcome, not how much came from policy, rising incomes or a changing mix of industry.

EPA's own 2011 estimate put the benefits of the 1990 Clean Air Act Amendments at more than 30 times their costs in its central case and about 3 times in its low case. It also projected over 230,000 early deaths prevented in 2020 alone. These are modeled projections, not measurements. [3]
