The Same Climate Policy Loses in 2050 and Wins by 2200

By Sam Scheinbach

How much does it matter which year you stop counting? Take one modeled climate policy, a worldwide carbon price that rises from about $6 a ton today to $90 by 2040 and $148 by 2060, and cut off its costs and benefits at different years.

The main finding

Climate policy trades costs now for benefits later, so the year you stop counting matters. The policy is a worldwide carbon price that every country adopts from 2025. It is the cost-benefit optimal path in the DICE-2023 model: about $6 per ton of CO2 today, $90 by 2040 and $148 by 2060 (2019 dollars), cutting emissions 40% by 2050 and 76% by 2100 compared with no controls. It is an idealized benchmark, not a real proposal: the authors call the assumption that every country joins at one harmonized price optimistic in the extreme. I compared it with the model's baseline, which keeps today's weak policy of about $6 a ton, rising 1% a year. [1] Costs come first and avoided damage later: the policy costs more than it saves until 2067, and the running total does not turn positive until 2101. [1]

So the verdict flips with the endpoint. Counting only to 2050, the policy shows a loss of about $5 trillion. To 2100 it roughly breaks even. To 2200 it shows a gain of about $51 trillion, and counting on to 2300 adds just $8 trillion more. At the model's discount rates, a dollar of benefit is worth 28 cents today if it arrives in 2050, 4.5 cents in 2100, and under a fifth of a cent in 2200.

By 2100 the model has counted 62% of the policy's lifetime costs but only 28% of its benefits. By 2300, costs total $50 trillion and avoided damage $109 trillion. [1]

The other side

Nearly all of the gain arrives after 2100, which is where the model is least reliable. DICE's damage function was calibrated for 1 to 4 °C of warming, yet its baseline reaches 5.4 °C by 2150, and the authors call damages the most uncertain part of the analysis. I also had to extend the model past 2150 by holding its values flat, so the late numbers are my assumptions, not model output. [1]

Where you stop counting is a choice, and it can change the answer.

The discount rate decides how long the future counts

Same policy, same costs and benefits, different discount rate. At 5% the total is nearly settled by 2200, and waiting until 2300 adds $0.5 trillion. At 2% it is still growing at 2300, up 51% from 2200. At 1% the total more than doubles between 2300 and 2520, from $3,920 trillion to $8,749 trillion, so the endpoint never stops mattering.

The 2% and 1% totals are dominated by the flat extension after 2150 and an out-of-range damage function. Read them as "the number keeps growing," not as forecasts. Holding the policy fixed is also a simplification: in DICE itself, a lower discount rate calls for a stronger policy. [1]

This is the old Stern and Nordhaus argument in miniature. The Stern Review used a 1.4% rate and Nordhaus about 4.3%, and Nordhaus argued the Review's conclusions depend decisively on its near-zero discounting. A $100 benefit 100 years away is worth $24.90 today at 1.4% and $1.48 at 4.3%. [3, 4, 5]

How the model works

Instead of running the full DICE-2023 model, I rebuilt its core from the numbers the paper publishes. Each year, the policy's extra abatement cost is subtracted from the damage it avoids, both as a share of world output, then converted to dollars and discounted. [1]

  • Published inputs: damage is 0.3467% of output times temperature squared; abatement cost is a coefficient times the control rate to the 2.6 power, with the coefficient falling from 10.9% of output to 2.7% in 2100; temperature and control-rate paths for the baseline and the optimal policy; a discount rate of 4.5% in 2020 falling to 3.4% in 2100; world output of $118 trillion in 2015 and $774 trillion in 2100. All money is in 2019 international dollars, which hold prices constant and adjust for what a dollar buys in each country. [1]
  • My assumptions: straight lines between the paper's table years; output growing 1% a year after 2100; control rates and abatement coefficients held flat after 2100; temperatures held at their 2150 values afterward; the discount rate falling in a straight line to 3.4% in 2100 and staying there.
  • Checks: from those inputs alone, the damage function reproduces the paper's own 2100 damages of 5.0% of output for the baseline and 2.6% for the optimal policy (I get 5.0% and 2.6%). My total through 2300 is $59 trillion, against the paper's own $107 trillion gain, which is the same order of magnitude but about half. [1, 2]
