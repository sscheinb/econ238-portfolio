# Why Can't America Build Anything? 

Introduction:

The United States wants to build more of almost everything right now: transmission lines, clean energy, semiconductor plants, even new nuclear reactors. But actually building things in America has become slow, expensive, and unpredictable in a way that's hard to explain with any single cause. Part of the problem is environmental review: a 1970 law called NEPA requires federal projects to study their environmental impact before breaking ground, and that process now averages years and invites lawsuits at nearly every step. Part of the problem may be bigger than environmental law at all. The U.S. produces roughly 35,000 new law graduates a year and only about 600 petroleum engineers, and some argue litigation itself has become a self-sustaining industry that slows down infrastructure regardless of which environmental rule is on the books. Meanwhile, other countries, China especially, build enormous infrastructure at a pace the U.S. hasn't matched in decades, though not without serious human costs of their own.

This page shares a real conversation exploring what's actually going on: is this a fixable regulatory problem, a deeper cultural one, or something in between? And if we could fix it, what would we have to give up to do it?
