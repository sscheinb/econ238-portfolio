A deck about ownership

Who actually owns the land?

A university can announce that its campus sits on stolen land and not change a single deed. This deck asks what that statement does, what it leaves alone, and what would actually change things.

Made from my recorded class discussion and written answers, Econ 238, University of Rochester. I used Claude to help organize it. The views are mine. Sources are on the last slide.

Start here

The sentence at the bottom of an email

"The University of Rochester is recognized as being located on the unceded territory of the Seneca Nation, a part of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy."

That line sits in the footer of university emails. It is called a land acknowledgment.

Pause. After someone reads it, what is different? Who gets something? Who gives something up?
Where it came from

A practice that grew without a law

  • Late 1970s, Australia: the "Welcome to Country" begins.
  • 1992: the Mabo decision recognizes native title in Australia, and acknowledgments spread through the 1990s.
  • 2015, Canada: after the Truth and Reconciliation Commission reports on residential schools, the practice grows.
  • US, 2010s and early 2020s: museums, universities and nonprofits adopt it. It gets wide attention at the 2020 Oscars.

What gave rise to it: reconciliation movements and public reports on colonial harm. What did not: a court ruling or law that changed who owns land.

The economics

It's cheap talk

Saying it costs almost nothing. It earns the speaker reputation. The people it names get very little.

Symbolic ownership

Saying the land is someone's.

Legal ownership

Who can sell it, build on it, borrow against it, and keep others off.

An acknowledgment only does the first. A critic quoted in one overview compares it to a thief who keeps a stolen laptop and adds a plaque admitting the theft. Others see it as a small first step.

Think about it. If a message costs nothing to send, what does it tell you about the sender?
The cost of disagreeing

Cheap to say, costly to question

At the University of Washington, computer science professor Stuart Reges put a mocking acknowledgment in his syllabus, saying that by "the labor theory of property" the Coast Salish people could claim almost none of the land the university sits on. The school ordered him to remove it, then investigated him.

On December 19, 2025, a divided federal appeals court held that the investigation and discipline threats violated his First Amendment rights. The court ruled on his right to speak. It did not say his property argument was right.

The equilibrium: agreeing is safe and earns praise, and disagreeing is risky. So most people say the line whatever they privately think, and it looks like everyone agrees. Economists call this preference falsification.

Think about it. When nobody objects, how would you know if anyone disagreed?
What "owning" means on a reservation

Sovereignty on paper, red tape in practice

A typical homeowner

  • Can sell
  • Can lease
  • Can borrow against it
  • Needs nobody's approval

Most reservation land

  • Held in trust by the federal government
  • Leases and mortgages usually need Bureau of Indian Affairs approval
  • Banks lend on leases, not the land

The General Allotment (Dawes) Act of 1887 split reservations into individual parcels of about 160 acres. When owners died, land passed to all heirs as shares, and today a single parcel can have hundreds of owners. A 2017 BIA estimate counted about 2.9 million of these fractional interests across 150 reservations, held by about 243,000 owners.

Think about it. If you can't sell, borrow against, or easily lease your land, how much is it worth to you?
A puzzle

Why Ireland and the Caymans, and not reservations?

Ireland and the Cayman Islands became corporate havens. Reservations have sovereignty, location and legal carve-outs, and mostly did not. The difference is rules a company can count on.

Ireland, Cayman Islands

  • Low or no income tax
  • Ireland: access to the European market
  • Cayman: a reliable legal system

Reservations

  • Trust land and slow approvals
  • Parcels with many owners
  • Unclear which courts and laws apply
  • Often far from cities and customers

A carve-out only helps if the basics work too.

The exception

Casinos: when the rules finally let investment in

In the 1987 Cabazon case, the Supreme Court held that states could not apply their gambling rules on reservations if they allowed gambling elsewhere. Congress passed the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act in 1988.

$100M → $16.7B → $26.5B+

Tribal gaming revenue: about $100 million in 1988, $16.7 billion in 2003, and over $26.5 billion a year since 2009.

My takeaway: casinos worked because the law gave tribes something valuable and reliable, and many are near customers. They did not fix property rights, and remote tribes cannot use that option. Where the rules let a reservation do something profitable, investment follows.

An example that works

Vancouver: build where you control the land

Noah Smith describes Sen̓áḵw, built by the Squamish Nation: about 11 towers and roughly 6,000 apartments on a little over 10 acres. It sits on reserve land, so it isn't under the city's zoning rules, and the Nation chose to build much denser than the city normally allows.

My view: this is a clear example of what real benefit looks like. When the owner can actually build on and earn from the land, the land has value. It ties back to the reservations that can't.

Think about it. What does it say about cities that a tribe can build what the city won't allow?
The big question

Give the national parks back?

The professor's proposal: return the parks to the tribes that were pushed out of them. The history, as told in a 2025 Wall Street Journal op-ed by Terry Anderson and Holly Fretwell:

  • Yellowstone (1872) took in part of the Crow reservation.
  • Yosemite pushed out the Southern Sierra Miwok.
  • The Blackfeet sold land in 1895 and lost hunting and fishing rights when Glacier became a park in 1910.
  • The Havasupai were removed from the Grand Canyon plateau in 1882 and regained 185,000 acres in 1975.
  • Ute Mountain Tribal Park charges $50 per person per day, versus $35 per car at Yellowstone.

My honest answer: it's a strong point, but I wouldn't hand over every park at once. I'd try co-management or leases with restrictions, such as no building a big store at a geyser, in a few parks, and measure results after ten years: tribal income, visitor numbers and how well the land is protected.

Hard questions I can't answer. Many tribes can claim one park. Could a tribe close it, or develop it? Who decides, and what would we measure?
What would change my mind

What would real follow-through look like?

Instead of a statement, an institution could offer:

  • Money or scholarships, such as tuition support for members of the Seneca Nation
  • Joint research or conservation projects, and hiring
  • Payment for the land it uses
  • Land returned, or a real say in how land is used

And a way to check: compare enrollment, graduation and funding before and after, and ask tribal leaders whether it helped. Why doesn't a school already do this? It costs money, and a statement is free. Donors and trustees aren't pushing for it.

Can I still make the case for symbolism? Yes. It costs little, it starts a conversation, and it brings up a history that was left out. I can accept that and still ask for more.

Your turn

Five questions to take with you

  1. Does an acknowledgment change who controls land? Is there a case where it did?
  2. If saying it is easy and objecting is risky, how would you know what people really think?
  3. Why did casinos work on reservations when so little else did?
  4. Would you give the national parks back to the tribes? All, some, or none, and why?
  5. If your school replaced its statement with an action, what should it be, and how would you measure it?
