Feed the Ocean Our Leftovers

Paying steelmakers to turn their waste into fish

Sam Scheinbach, ECON 238, Week 11

The idea

Steelmaking leaves behind slag, a man-made rock rich in iron that costs mills money to get rid of. Plankton need iron to grow, and some coasts and parts of the ocean are short of it. My idea is for steel companies to ship prepared slag to iron-poor fishing grounds, run through a local fishing cooperative that owns the right to the extra fish. More iron means more plankton, more plankton means more fish, and the steelmaker gets a use for its waste. It would start with small coastal pilots.

Why it seems crazy, and why it may not be

It seems crazy because it means putting industrial waste in the sea on purpose. At least 16 open ocean iron experiments have been done, and one critical summary says none has shown it to be an effective way to remove carbon (Geoengineering Monitor, which opposes the technique).

It may not be crazy because the coastal version already exists. In 2004 Nippon Steel developed a method that mixes steel slag with soil and humus to restore dying seaweed beds at Mashike, Hokkaido. Kelp grew well 30 meters from the units after six months, and the effect was confirmed after two and three years. But the proven part is small: the project’s certified carbon removal was only 49.5 tons between 2018 and 2022.

The calculation

Plankton need very little iron: about 5 micromoles per mole of carbon for open ocean algae (13 to 16 for coastal diatoms), so one ton of iron supports about 43,000 tons of plankton carbon. I assumed fish are 10 percent carbon and each step up the food chain keeps 10 percent of the energy, with three steps from plankton to a fish people catch. That means one ton of fish needs about 100 tons of plankton carbon, or about 2.3 kg of usable iron.

How much slag that takes depends on how much of its iron plankton can use. I assumed slag is 15 percent iron. A Nippon Steel paper says dissolved iron from slag quickly turns into an insoluble solid, which “markedly decreases the biological utility of iron,” so I tested four cases.

Slag costs $5 per ton at the plant plus about $22.50 shipping (rounded from bulk freight examples of $9.65 and $34.25 per ton). Fish are worth about $1,720 per ton, which I calculated from FAO 2022 data.

At that fish price, the project breaks even if plankton can use about 0.025 percent of the iron in slag, or about 0.25 percent if only 1 in 10 extra fish gets caught. That is a low bar but not zero, and it is something a pilot can test.

To check for overdoing it, the world makes 190 to 290 million tons of steel slag a year (USGS). Using 10 percent of it at 0.1 percent usability adds about 1.2 to 1.9 million tons of fish, 1.5 to 2.3 percent of the roughly 81 million ton marine catch. At 1 percent usability it adds 15 to 23 percent. At 10 percent the math says more than the entire world catch, which is impossible, so iron must stop being the limit first, and the other nutrients (nitrogen, silica) set the real ceiling.

Why hasn’t someone done this already?

Today slag is sold as road base, asphalt filler and a cement ingredient, or landfilled. That is predictable and legal. Its shortcoming is that it does nothing for iron-starved coasts and leaves steelmakers with a disposal cost.

Four things stand in the way. It is mostly illegal: the London Protocol was amended in 2013 to ban ocean fertilization except for legitimate scientific research. The one famous commercial attempt went badly, when the Haida Salmon Restoration Corporation dumped 120 tons of ferrous sulphate off British Columbia in 2012 and the project was cancelled after an international outcry. The carbon results from experiments were disappointing. And nobody owns the open ocean, so a company cannot capture the fish its iron creates. That is why I tie the project to a fishing cooperative with a clear right to its grounds.

The incumbents hurt would be buyers of slag. Cement and road builders use it as a cheap input, and diverting it to sea could raise their costs and, for cement, mean more limestone and more CO2 elsewhere.

The Chesterton’s fence question

Low iron in some waters is a natural limit, and removing it changes the food web. Critics warn of toxic algal blooms, oxygen loss when a bloom decays, and modeling that predicts harm to fisheries, which is the opposite of what I promise. It is worth the risk only because I start with small coastal tests that can be measured and stopped. The fence only comes down if the pilots show the benefit is real.

Incentives and unintended effects

The steelmaker saves its disposal cost. The cooperative pays for shipping out of the extra catch, or they split it. Unintended effects I expect are a rise in costs for slag buyers, possible toxic blooms, a pH rise near the site (slag releases calcium), and possible metals leaching, which I could not find a source on. If it were sold as a climate fix, cheap weak carbon credits could also reduce the pressure to cut emissions. External effects reach neighbors who fish the same water, for good if the fish spread, and for bad if a bloom turns toxic.

The biggest drawbacks

The technical one is that we do not know how much slag iron plankton can use, and the break-even depends on it. The fix is a small pilot that measures it, using the humus method from Japan. The political one is that the law bans it and the Haida case hurt trust. The fix is to run pilots under a scientific permit, with the community’s consent and shared catch.
